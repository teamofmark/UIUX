South Korea, a Deep-sea Fishing Powerhouse, Designated as an Illegal Fishing Nation and Its Escape Story
[작성자 부분]
Text by: Park Tae-hyun, Marine Protection Campaigner at Greenpeace Seoul Office

[문단 - 배경강조]
Over the past two years, Korea’s distant-water fishing industry faced a major crisis as it was designated as an illegal fishing nation and a preliminary illegal fishing nation by the United States and the European Union. Leading up to the lifting of the illegal fishing designation by the U.S. and the EU in February and April 2015, respectively, there were significant changes, including extensive legal amendments and reform measures for the development of the distant-water fishing industry. Throughout this entire process, Greenpeace raised its voice to urge the prevention of illegal fishing in Korea.

#1. Illegal fishing by Korean deep-sea fishing vessels in various global waters exposed
    [문단 - 01]
    <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/feature-story/2/2014/419893/">Engaging in illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) fishing</a> directly leads to a loss of international standing. Not only is such behavior inconsistent with international norms for Korea, an OECD member, but it is also an outdated practice that depletes public resources shared by everyone for the short-term gains of a few. The revelation of illegal fishing activities by Korean distant-water vessels, followed by the designation of Korea as an illegal fishing nation by the United States and a preliminary illegal fishing nation by the European Union, has clearly demonstrated that Korea is not yet taking the lead in addressing the shared challenges of the 21st century.

    [문단 - 02]
    Illegal fishing is <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/feature-story/2/2014/431830/">a destructive fishing practice that threatens marine ecosystems, plunders resources from coastal nations, and</a> is often linked to dirty industrial practices such as frequent human rights violations against crew members. There are as many as 34 cases listed in <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/multimedia/publications/2013/oceans/353504/">the report on the state of illegal fishing published by Greenpeace in April 2013 alone. Illegal fishing</a> in Korea was not merely the fault of a few distant-water fishing vessels, but a structural problem within the distant-water fishing industry that was rampant across oceans worldwide, targeting everything from tuna and toothfish to migratory fish. It was even more shocking <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/feature-story/2/2014/4317061/">to discover that familiar, representative Korean companies</a> were among those involved in illegal fishing.

#2. Criticism from the International Community, and Eventually Designated as an Illegal Fishing Nation
    [문단 - 01]
    Korea, a ‘deep-sea fishing powerhouse’ with 342 <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/update/5894/blog-ocean-korea-efforts-to-clear-illegal-fishing-status/#_ftn1">[1] fishing vessels in the world’s five oceans, has taken measures that do not meet international standards as a flag state despite the numerous illegal fishing activities caused by the deep-sea fishing industry. It has tried to cover up the situation</a><a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/press-release/oceans/2013/353503/">with lenient punishments that do not correspond to the scale of the crime, silence</a> , and even <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/press-release/oceans/2014/422521/">opposition from local fisheries management organizations to registering fishing vessels that engaged in illegal fishing on the illegal fishing vessel list</a>.

    [문단 - 02]
    Although illegal fishing is not committed only by the Korean industry, the Korean government has shown a lenient attitude toward illegal fishing by its distant-water fishing industry over the past few years, which has led many governments, including the United States, to take notice of the Korean government's inadequate measures and the level of sanctions against illegal fishing. Consequently, in <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/feature-story/2/2014/429530/">January 2013, the United States placed Korea on the list of illegal fishing nations along with nine other countries, including Colombia, Ecuador, and Ghana</a>, and in November of the same year, the European Union also designated <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/press-release/oceans/2013/388618/">Korea as a preliminary illegal fishing nation due to the Korean government's inadequate control over illegal fishing .</a>

    [이미지 - 02]


    [문단 - 03]
    The designation of Korea as a nation engaging in illegal fishing dealt a severe blow to Korea's standing on the international stage. In particular, among the countries also designated as such, Korea possesses infrastructure and economic power, along with a long history of developing its deep-sea fishing industry; therefore, the loss of national prestige resulting from this designation was felt as a particularly grave issue.

    [문단 - 04]
    The damage caused by being designated as an illegal fishing nation does not end merely with international disgrace. Both the United States and the European Union have established appropriate legal frameworks to prevent illegal fishing; they identify countries that fail to make efforts toward management and conservation by regional fisheries management bodies <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/press-release/oceans/2013/388618/">and impose sanctions, such as trade restrictions on seafood within the U.S. and EU, as well as bans on the use of ports for all vessels from those countries</a>. In particular, being designated as an illegal fishing nation by the EU carries the added risk of nullifying existing fisheries agreements. The resulting economic losses are truly enormous.

#3. Greenpeace Launches Campaign to Help Korea Escape Illegal Fishing Status
    [문단 - 01]
    The first step to solving a problem is to accurately recognize it. In April 2013, Greenpeace exposed the inappropriate and illegal fishing practices of South Korean distant-water fishing vessels through a report. Through this, we reaffirmed to the public and the international community that illegal fishing in Korea is a widespread practice and a serious issue that must be taken seriously. This also marked the beginning of calls for reform from the South Korean government and the distant-water fishing industry.
    [문단 - 02]
    We also urged an investigation through <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/press-release/oceans/2013/357013/">peaceful direct action in front of the Dongwon tuna fishing vessel Premier, which is under investigation for illegal fishing in Liberian waters . We also confirmed to citizens</a><a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/feature-story/2/2014/4297131/">that the Inseong 7 and 3, Inseong Industries' toothfish fishing vessels that repeatedly violated Argentina's exclusive waters, were "illegal fishing vessels" by carrying out peaceful direct action at the Port of Montevideo, Uruguay and Gamcheon Port, Busan, respectively .</a>

    [이미지 - 03]


    [문단 - 03]
    Even if it was the deep-sea fishing vessels that committed illegal fishing, the more fundamental problem lies in the fact that the Korean government prioritized the interests of the industry over the public and national interests. Outdated laws and regulations regarding the deep-sea fishing industry, along with a level of awareness that was insensitive to or lagging behind the international community's efforts to sustainably manage marine resources, were areas that urgently needed reform.

    [문단 - 04]
    In May 2013, Greenpeace <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/feature-story/2/20131/357766/">held a National Assembly policy workshop with officials from various government ministries to present the direction for the future of Korea's distant-water fisheries policy</a>. Starting with this, we have continuously made proposals to improve the system throughout the two revision processes of the Distant-Water Industry Development Act. From analyzing the first amendment and <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/multimedia/publications/2014/oceans/420498/">delivering reform proposals to the government</a>to address its shortcomings , to analyzing loopholes in the revised bill after the first amendment and <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/multimedia/publications/2014/oceans/434518/">proposing stronger legislation , to delivering</a> <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/multimedia/publications/2015/oceans/opinion-on-distant-water-fisheries/">written opinions on amendments to subordinate regulations</a> for the proper implementation of the Distant-Water Industry Development Act , Greenpeace has consistently presented solutions to enable Korea to eradicate illegal fishing and move toward legislation that meets international standards for sustainable fisheries.

#4. The Finally Revised Deep-Sea Fisheries Development Act, and the Challenges Still Remaining After the Lifting of the Illegal Fishing Nation Designation 
    [문단 - 01]
    <a href="https://www.greenpeace.org/korea/news/press-release/oceans/2015/436862/">The Act on the Development of the Distant-Sea Fisheries Industry, promulgated in January 2015, marks the first step in a long journey to structurally improve the industry, which continues to operate based on a model from half a century ago.</a> In recognition of these efforts to overhaul the system, the United States and the European Union decided to lift the designation of Korea as a nation engaging in illegal fishing. This decision was made because they recognized the reform of key laws related to the distant-sea fishing industry, as well as the strengthening of supervision, control, and surveillance of distant-sea fishing vessels, as positive changes. The cooperation among the government, the distant-sea fishing industry, and NGOs during this process served as a crucial foundation for these positive changes. The significance of this achievement was even greater because there was collaboration among everyone striving to resolve issues from their respective positions.

This is equivalent to about 15% of the world's total fish catch [2] .
